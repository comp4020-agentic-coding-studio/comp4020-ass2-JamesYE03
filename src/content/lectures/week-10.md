Trust and safety work used to mean a large team of human reviewers applying a
policy document to a queue. That model is breaking under its own volume, and
what's replacing it is a hybrid most platforms are still improvising:
AI-assisted triage feeding human judgement on the genuinely hard cases, a
labelling regime for synthetic content that varies wildly between platforms,
and a disclosure requirement that only works if creators comply with it
voluntarily. A 2025 conference paper put the uncomfortable question in its
title: is this "the end of trust and safety" as a distinct discipline, or the
beginning of a more specialised one?

We will read one real platform's synthetic-media labelling policy line by
line, then write a better one for a case that policy doesn't cover cleanly.
YouTube's actual disclosure policy is a good specimen: creators self-report
"AI use" during upload, realistic synthetic content requires disclosure while
non-realistic content or minor edits don't, the platform can apply a label
automatically and notify the creator when it does, and certain sensitive
categories — elections, health, major public events — get a more prominent
label than the rest. The rule of thumb underneath all of it: if a viewer
could reasonably believe this really happened, it needs a label.

## Outline

- from scaled moderation to specialised review: what changed and why
- disclosure and labelling regimes, compared across platforms
- the cases every current policy handles badly: satire, research, consented
  synthetic likeness
