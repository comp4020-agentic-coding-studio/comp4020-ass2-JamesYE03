In early 2024, a finance employee at Arup — the British engineering firm
behind buildings like the Sydney Opera House — joined a video call at the
firm's Hong Kong office with several senior colleagues, including the CFO,
and was instructed to transfer roughly USD 25 million. Every person on that
call except the employee was a synthetic reconstruction; Arup later
confirmed the incident to CNN. Later that year, a deepfake video
impersonating a well-known entrepreneur was used to promote a cryptocurrency
scheme that persuaded an elderly retiree to invest his life savings. Neither
case needed a research-grade model — commodity tools were enough, which is
the actual headline.

This lecture covers how synthetic video and cloned audio are made well enough
to survive a live call, what a forensic review of a recording can and can't
recover after the fact, and why "just look closely" stopped being useful
advice for anyone outside a research lab.

## Outline

- the two cases, and what made each of them work
- synthesis versus reenactment: different techniques, different tells
- what a post-hoc forensic review can check, and what it can't
