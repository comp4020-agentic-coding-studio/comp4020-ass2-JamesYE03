Every method in this course can be pointed at an innocent person. A
distinctive human writing style can trip a text detector. A real photograph,
badly compressed, can trip an image one. A student's unmedicated tremor can
read, to an untrained reviewer, like a synthetic voice's artefacts. A forensic
practice that only worries about missing the guilty and never about convicting
the innocent is not a forensic practice — it's a witch-hunt with better
instruments.

This lecture borrows the presumption of innocence and the burden of proof
directly from actual criminal procedure, not as a metaphor but as a working
standard: what would it take for you to be *confident* in an accusation, not
just suspicious, and what do you owe someone when you're wrong. It sets up the
week's casework, which asks you to argue both sides of a real provenance
dispute before you're allowed to reach a verdict.

## Outline

- false positives have a face: what a wrongful "this is AI" accusation costs
