# 99.3% accuracy. Also: 80–92% accuracy.

## Same class of tool. Different measurement.

---

## Two numbers, one detector

- **vendor's own benchmark**: 99.3% accuracy across 3,000 samples, 0.24%
  false-positive rate
- **independent reviewers**, same class of tool: 80–92% in real-world use,
  falling to roughly 96.5% even for a strong performer once the text is
  paraphrased or lightly edited rather than generated clean

---

## Neither number is "wrong"

They're measuring different things: a clean benchmark versus content that's
been through exactly the kind of editing real writing goes through. The gap
between them is itself the lesson.

---

{/* _class: quote */}

Goodhart's Law: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good
measure.

---

## Applied here

The moment a detector's verdict starts costing someone a grade, a payment or
a job, someone builds a product whose only purpose is to remove the signal
that verdict depends on. That's not a bug in any one detector. It's what
happens to any single signal once enough weight rests on it.

## The practice, not the tool

No detector deserves to be the whole case. Multi-signal review — text, image,
provenance, context — is the only defensible practice, and even that isn't a
guarantee.
